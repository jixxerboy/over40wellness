Glucosamine and Collagen After 40: Do They Really Help Your Joints?

8 November 2026  ·  6 min read

← Back to Blog

Walk down any health aisle and you'll see them: glucosamine tablets, collagen powders, "joint support" blends, often at a fair price each month. Millions of people take them. But when you look at the research, the two ingredients tell quite different stories.

Here's what the evidence says for each, who needs to be careful, and what's likely to help your joints more.

What They Are

Glucosamine is a natural substance found in cartilage, the smooth cushioning at the ends of your bones. Supplements are usually made from shellfish shells. It's often sold with chondroitin, another component of cartilage.

Collagen is the main structural protein in your body, and supplements usually come from animal or fish sources. The idea behind both sounds logical: take what your joints are made of. Your body doesn't work quite that simply, though, because what you swallow gets broken down first.

Glucosamine: The Evidence Is Weak

Glucosamine has been studied for decades, and the results have been disappointing. In the UK, NICE tells doctors not to offer it for osteoarthritis, and says there's no strong evidence that it helps.

The GAIT trial (US): One of the best-known studies tested glucosamine, chondroitin and the two together against a placebo in people with knee osteoarthritis. The first results found modest benefit at most, and over two years none of them made a statistically significant difference to pain.

Some studies of one particular form, glucosamine sulphate, have looked better, but the results overall haven't been consistent. Plenty of people still say they feel a difference, and it does seem to be reasonably safe for most. It's just that good trials haven't been able to show it reliably beats a dummy pill.

Collagen: More Promising, Not Proven

Collagen has a better recent track record, at least for people who already have osteoarthritis.

Pooled research (2024): A review in the journal Osteoarthritis and Cartilage combined 25 trials and 2,856 patients. Collagen products had small-to-moderate benefits for pain and function compared with a placebo or control, and didn't cause more side effects.

There are reasons to be cautious. Another review, from 2023, also found less knee pain but rated all of its trials at high risk of bias. The trials used different types and amounts of collagen, so it's hard to know what to buy. A trial in adults without arthritis found no improvement in knee pain over 12 weeks. And when the European Food Safety Authority looked at a collagen joint health claim back in 2011, it concluded that a benefit hadn't been established, although much of today's research came later.

So some people with osteoarthritis may get a modest benefit. It's not a way of rebuilding worn cartilage, and there's little to suggest it does much for healthy joints.

Who Should Check First

  • If you take warfarin, avoid glucosamine. The UK medicines regulator advises against it, because of reports of it strengthening warfarin's effect. Ask about other blood thinners too.
  • If you have a shellfish allergy, speak to a pharmacist before taking glucosamine. Many products are made from shellfish, and expert opinion on the risk varies.
  • If you have diabetes, check first. There's limited data, and you may be advised to monitor your blood sugar more closely.
  • If you're vegetarian or vegan, check the source, because most collagen comes from animals or fish.
  • If you're pregnant, breastfeeding or on regular medicines, ask your GP or pharmacist before starting any supplement.

If You Decide to Try One

Many people want to find out for themselves, which is fair enough. If that's you, write down how your joints feel before you start, give it around three months, and be honest with yourself at the end. If nothing has changed, stop paying for it. And choose a product from a well-known retailer that says clearly what's in it and how much.

What's Likely to Help More

NICE's core treatments for osteoarthritis aren't in a bottle. They're therapeutic exercise and, if you're carrying extra weight, losing some of it. Strengthening the muscles around a joint takes some of the strain off it. Our guide to knee pain on the stairs has three simple exercises to start with, and there's more on foods that may help joint pain.

The Bottom Line

Glucosamine has had a long time to prove itself, and the evidence still isn't convincing. Collagen looks more promising for people with osteoarthritis, but the benefit seems modest and the research is messy. Neither is a replacement for keeping your joints moving and your muscles strong.

This article is for general information only and isn't medical advice. Always check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting a new supplement, especially if you take regular medicines.
