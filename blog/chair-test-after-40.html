The Chair Test: Can You Stand Up Without Your Hands?

11 October 2026  ·  4 min read

← Back to Blog

Try this right now: sit on a dining chair, cross your arms over your chest, and stand up without using your hands. 🪑

Easy? Wobbly? Couldn't do it?

That simple movement says more about your health than most people realise. Standing up from a chair without help needs strong legs, good balance and muscles that react quickly. Those are exactly the things that keep us independent as we get older, and that help us catch ourselves when we trip on a kerb.

Why Such a Simple Test Matters

Doctors and physiotherapists use chair-stand tests to check leg strength and balance, especially when looking at the risk of falls. Research has also linked how easily people get up from a chair or the floor with how well they age overall.

The reason is simple: your thigh and hip muscles are the biggest in your body, and they're the ones that shrink fastest when we sit more and move less. You use them every time you get out of bed, climb the stairs or step off a bus.

How to Do the 30-Second Chair Test Safely

  1. Use a sturdy chair without wheels, with its back against a wall so it can't slide.
  2. Sit in the middle of the seat, feet flat on the floor, about hip-width apart.
  3. Cross your arms over your chest.
  4. Stand up fully, then sit back down. That's one.
  5. Count how many you can do in 30 seconds, at a steady pace.

If you feel unsteady, have someone with you, or do it next to a worktop you can grab. Stop if anything hurts.

What Your Result Means

There's no pass or fail, and the numbers vary with age. As a rough guide, the US health service's falls-prevention programme (CDC STEADI) treats fewer than about 12 stands in 30 seconds as below average for people in their early 60s, and the expected number drops gradually with age.

In your 40s and 50s, you'd usually hope to do comfortably more than that. If you can't stand up at all without using your hands, that's a sign it's worth working on your leg strength now, while it's easiest to build.

Most importantly, write down your number. Then try again in a month and see it improve.

Strength Isn't Everything: Speed Matters Too

Strength is how much force your muscles make. Power is how quickly they make it. Power tends to fade faster than strength as we age, and it's power that helps you react when you stumble.

That's why the best chair-stand practice is to sit down slowly and stand up fast. You're training strength and speed at the same time.

How to Get Better at It (No Gym Needed)

  • Daily chair stands: one or two sets of 5–10, sitting down slowly and standing up briskly.
  • Twice-weekly strength sessions: squats (or chair stands), step-ups on the bottom stair, press-ups (against a wall or worktop to start), and rows with a resistance band.
  • Walk briskly most days, and take the stairs when you can.
  • Eat enough protein (eggs, fish, beans, yoghurt, meat) to help your muscles recover and grow.

Muscle responds to training at any age. People in their 70s and 80s still get stronger with regular practice, so in your 40s and 50s you're in a great position to get ahead.

If you have joint problems, heart or blood pressure issues, dizziness or a history of falls, check with your GP or a physiotherapist before starting new exercises.
